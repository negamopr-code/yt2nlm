# Why You Forget New Words in Days — and the 15-Minute Sentence Method That Makes Them Stick

You found a great word on Monday. You wrote it down. You even said it twice.

By Friday, it's gone.

Not "on the tip of your tongue" gone. *Gone* gone.

If that sounds familiar, you're in very good company. Across thousands of comments from people learning English, the same sentence shows up again and again:

> "I can understand 100% of the video, but when I try to speak, my mind goes blank."

Here's the good news: **your memory is not broken.** You're using a method that is built to fail. Let's fix it — in about 15 minutes a day, with no app required.

---

## 1. Your brain deletes words on a schedule

In 1885, psychologist Hermann Ebbinghaus memorized lists and tested how much he lost over time. His famous **forgetting curve** looks like this:

| Time after learning | Share forgotten |
|---|---|
| 1 hour | about **56%** |
| 1 day | about **66%** |
| 6 days | about **75%** |

So yes: without a plan, you can lose **three out of four** new words within a week.

But notice one detail. Ebbinghaus memorized *meaningless* syllables — things like "DAX" and "BOK" — with nothing to connect them to. Material with meaning fades more slowly.

**That is the whole secret.** The more a word is *attached* to something, the longer it survives.

---

## 2. The "word in isolation" trap

Most of us learn vocabulary like this:

> **livid** = very angry

That's a *word in isolation*: a label with nothing holding it in place. Your brain files it as noise and clears it out.

Now compare:

> *"He was **livid** when he found out his flight had been cancelled — for the third time."*

Suddenly the word has a situation, a person, a feeling and a reason. That sentence gives your memory four hooks instead of one. And when you need the word in a real conversation, you don't have to *build* a sentence from nothing — you already own one.

**Rule 1: Never learn a word alone. Learn it inside a sentence.**

---

## 3. Re-reading feels productive. It isn't.

Here's the uncomfortable part. Looking at your word list again *feels* like studying — but it mostly trains recognition ("oh yes, I know that one"), not recall ("what's the word for…?").

In a well-known 2006 study, Roediger and Karpicke had students either **re-read** a text or **practise recalling** it. A week later, the re-readers remembered about **40%**. The students who practised recalling remembered about **61%**.

Same time spent. Far better result. The difference: **pulling the word out of your head, instead of pushing it in again.**

**Rule 2: Cover the answer. Make your brain fetch it.**

Try it right now. Cover the right column and say the upgrade out loud:

| Everyday phrase | Stronger word (cover this!) |
|---|---|
| very angry | livid |
| very busy | swamped |
| very tired | exhausted |
| very happy | thrilled |
| very important | crucial |

That little moment of effort — *"wait… it's… swamped!"* — is exactly the moment the memory gets stronger.

---

## 4. Timing beats effort: the 1 · 3 · 7 rule

Recall works best just before you'd forget. You don't need a complicated app for that — just three short check-ins:

- **Day 1** — see the word again in its sentence.
- **Day 3** — cover it and recall it before you look.
- **Day 7** — use it in a new sentence of your own, out loud.

Each check-in resets the forgetting curve — and every time, it falls more slowly than before. After day 7, a word you've used yourself is very hard to lose.

**Rule 3: Review on days 1, 3 and 7 — not every day, not never.**

---

## 5. Know the word's "setting" (or you'll freeze anyway)

Many learners freeze mid-sentence not because they forgot the word, but because they're unsure it *fits*. Is it too formal? Too rude? Too dramatic?

That doubt costs you the word at the worst moment. So learn one more thing with every word — its **register** and when to **avoid** it:

**swamped** — *overwhelmed with work*
- Register: casual to professional, fine at work
- Example: *"I'm completely swamped with reports this week — can we talk on Monday?"*
- Avoid when: you only have one or two small tasks

**livid** — *extremely angry*
- Register: neutral, a bit dramatic
- Example: *"She was livid when the landlord kept her deposit."*
- Avoid when: someone is just slightly annoyed

**let me look into that** — *a polite promise to find out*
- Register: professional, perfect for emails and meetings
- Example: *"I don't have the numbers right now, but let me look into that and get back to you today."*
- Avoid when: chatting with friends about personal preferences

That's why every word on **anotherwordfor.net** comes with an example sentence and a note on when to use it. Confidence, not just vocabulary.

**Rule 4: Learn the setting, not just the meaning.**

---

## 6. The 15-minute routine (copy this)

Do this once a day. Three new words is plenty.

**Minutes 0–5 — Anchor (new words)**
Pick 3 words. For each, write **one sentence about your own life** that uses it, plus its register.

**Minutes 5–10 — Recall (words from day 1 and day 3)**
Cover the words. Read your "everyday phrase" column. Say the stronger word before you look.

**Minutes 10–15 — Speak (words from day 7)**
Say two new sentences out loud with each word, as if you were telling a friend or a colleague.

That's it. No streaks to protect, no decks to manage. Just sentences, recall and your own voice.

---

## 7. Why this also fixes the "blank mind" when speaking

The blank-mind moment happens when your brain tries to do three jobs at once: find the word, check if it fits, and build the grammar around it — while someone is waiting for you.

The sentence method removes two of those jobs in advance. You practised the word in a full sentence, you know its setting, and you've already said it out loud. So in the real conversation, the word arrives with a ready-made sentence attached.

You don't need a better memory. You need **words that come with their sentences.**

---

## Quick recap

1. Words learned in isolation fade on a schedule — about 75% within a week.
2. Learn every word **inside a sentence**.
3. **Cover and recall** instead of re-reading.
4. Review on **days 1, 3 and 7**.
5. Learn each word's **register** and when to avoid it.
6. **Say it out loud** in your own sentence.
