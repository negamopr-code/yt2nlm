# S3 — Synonym Memory Challenge: BIG (script v1)

Language gate: APPROVED 2026-09-28 after fixes (see lang-gate-v1.md).

(Written without a leading `>` on purpose: in this script `>` marks SPOKEN lines.)

Series: Synonym Memory Challenge · Special S3 · format: mid-length 16:9 explainer, whiteboard style, target 5–7 min.
Method (for us only, never in the voice-over): list test → pause → one continuous visual story → pause → "15, not 10" reveal → comment your two scores → try it with your own words.

Conventions:
- Lines starting with `>` are SPOKEN. Everything else (headings, [brackets], notes) is for us / the drawing, not spoken. Never read a [bracket] aloud.
- [EXTRA] marks the 5 "sneaky" extras that are in the story but NOT in the list. Never say "extra" in the story itself.
- [Stress cue] = ON-SCREEN text only, next to the word's caption. It is never spoken and never drilled (S1 lesson).
- Timings are approximate (0.9× reading pace, ~140 spoken words/min).
- Tone: light and silly. Nobody is hurt, no real people, no real brands. The story has a happy ending.
- ONE main character in every scene: "you" = the same person, same clothes (yellow raincoat, green wellies) — S2 lesson: the character changed between a girl and a man.
- EVERY one of the 15 words gets its own picture (S2 lesson: dejected was a caption on a blank background).

Lessons from S1/S2 applied up front: no spoken pronunciation drills; no stage directions read aloud; real silent holds on both pause cards (planned for polish anyway); recap = exactly 15 rows, one row per word, no pairs, exactly the Beat 5 labels; no "top/bottom of the ladder" in the voice (S2: the drawn table was reversed) — the voice says nothing about "top/bottom" or a "strongest" word (gate: ginormous = "informal and playful"); the reveal makes no "came only from the story" claim; the comment line is one exact sentence ("Which score was higher? Comment your two scores: list versus story.") because NotebookLM dropped it twice; a transfer line ("tonight, try it with your own new words") before the CTA; tip "walk through the story again" (not "back through"); CTA = look up "another word for big" on anotherwordfor.net (no search box, no example sentences on the site).

## Beat 3 — Visual story: one pumpkin, 15 words, big → extremely big (0:50–4:45)

[Screen: one continuous whiteboard story (a series of panels is fine, as long as the story runs on and "you" is the same person in the yellow raincoat); each word is written next to its picture as it is spoken, with its one-line meaning. The pumpkin visibly grows from scene to scene.]

> Now the trick: pictures instead of a list. One story — just imagine every scene. It goes roughly from big to extremely big, and so do the words.

### 1. large
[Garden-centre café: a menu board "SMALL · MEDIUM · LARGE"; you holding a large coffee; a poster on the wall: "VILLAGE VEGETABLE SHOW — SATURDAY".]
> Large. You want to win the village vegetable show. So you go to the garden centre and order a large coffee. Large — big. We use it for sizes, like on menus and clothes: "a large coffee". And for amounts: "a large number of people". It's a bit more formal than big.

### 2. considerable
[You at a café table, reading a book as thick as a brick: "GROWING PUMPKINS — 800 pages"; the clock on the wall has moved from morning to night; a pile of empty coffee cups.]
> Considerable. You pick up one pumpkin seed and a book with eight hundred pages. You sit down with more coffee. Reading it takes a considerable amount of time: when you look up, it's dark outside. Considerable — large in amount. Fairly formal, and we often use it with time, money and effort.

### 3. bulky [EXTRA]
[You walking out with a box of plant food as big as a sofa, a strange shape; you can't see over it; you bump into a lamppost.]
> Bulky. You also buy a box of plant food. It's as big as a sofa and a very strange shape. You can't see over it, and on the way out you walk straight into a lamppost. Bulky — big and difficult to carry or move: "a bulky box", "a bulky suitcase".

### 4. hefty [EXTRA]
[At the till: a very long receipt rolling across the floor; the total circled; you with your mouth open, wallet empty.]
> Hefty. At the till, the receipt rolls across the floor and out of the door. It's a hefty bill. Hefty — big and heavy. With money, bigger than usual, and a bit informal: "a hefty bill", "a hefty fine".

### 5. jumbo
[Evening at home: you in an armchair in front of a flowerpot, eating from a jumbo pack of popcorn (the pack is nearly as tall as you), watching the soil like a film.]
> Jumbo. At home you plant the seed in a flowerpot. Then you open a jumbo pack of popcorn and watch the flowerpot all night, like a film. Jumbo — larger than usual. Informal, and we use it for products: "a jumbo pack".

### 6. huge
[Morning: the flowerpot broken; a pumpkin the size of an armchair in your living room; you in pyjamas, cup in hand, shocked.]
> Huge. Next morning, you wake up in the armchair. The pot is broken, and there's a huge pumpkin in your living room. Huge — extremely big. Very common in speech: "a huge mistake".

### 7. giant
[Garden: your pumpkin next to your neighbour's normal pumpkins, which look like peas; a phone showing the local newspaper's website, headline "GIANT PUMPKIN IN MILL LANE".]
> Giant. You roll it into the garden. Next to it, your neighbour's pumpkins look like peas. The local newspaper puts it online: "Giant pumpkin in Mill Lane!" Giant — much bigger than others of its kind. Only before a noun: "a giant pumpkin".

### 8. enormous
[The pumpkin as big as a car; your cat climbing it with a rope and a flag, like a mountain.]
> Enormous. By lunch it's as big as a car. Your cat climbs it with a rope, like a mountain. Enormous — extremely big. A neutral word: fine in speech and in writing.

### 9. massive
[You pushing the pumpkin with all your strength; it doesn't move; your wheelbarrow flattened under it like a pancake.]
> Massive. You try to push it into the shade, but it doesn't move at all. And your wheelbarrow is under it — as flat as a pancake. Massive — very big, heavy and solid, like a rock.

### 10. whopping [EXTRA]
[A farmer's scale on the back of a truck; the display reads "1,200 kg"; the farmer's hat flying off in surprise.]
> Whopping. A farmer arrives with a scale on his truck. The pumpkin weighs a whopping twelve hundred kilos! Whopping — very big. Informal, and often right before a number: "a whopping twelve hundred kilos".

### 11. vast
[Aerial view: huge pumpkin leaves covering your garden, the neighbours' gardens, the football pitch and the car park — a green sea; tiny people waving from rooftops.]
> Vast. Its leaves spread over your garden, all the neighbours' gardens and the football pitch. From the church tower, it looks like a vast green sea. Vast — extremely big, for areas, spaces and amounts: "a vast field", "the vast majority".

### 12. towering [EXTRA]
[The pumpkin now taller than your house; its shadow covering the street; a bird's nest on top; you looking up, tiny.]
[Stress cue, on screen only: TOW-er-ing]
> Towering. By evening, the pumpkin is taller than your house, and a bird builds a nest on top. Everyone in the street stops and looks up at this towering pumpkin. Towering — very tall. A bit literary, and only before a noun: "towering trees", "towering cliffs".

### 13. mammoth [EXTRA]
[Saturday: the whole village — fifty neighbours, three tractors and one very small dog — pulling ropes to move the pumpkin down the road to the show.]
> Mammoth. Saturday. Moving it to the show is a mammoth task: fifty neighbours, three tractors and one very small dog, all pulling ropes. Mammoth — extremely big, usually before a noun. We often say "a mammoth task" or "a mammoth job".

### 14. immense
[The pumpkin arriving at the show ground in one piece; you sitting on the grass, a big sigh bubble "Phew!"; the neighbours cheering.]
[Stress cue, on screen only: im-MENSE]
> Immense. After six hours, it arrives at the show in one piece. You sit down on the grass and feel immense relief. Immense — extremely big or great. We also use it for feelings: "immense relief", "immense pride".

### 15. ginormous
[A little girl at the show pointing up at the pumpkin, mouth wide open, speech bubble "It's GINORMOUS!"]
> Ginormous. A little girl looks up and shouts: "It's ginormous!" Ginormous — extremely big. Informal and playful: for chatting with friends, not for work emails.

### Happy ending (no new word)
[A first-prize rosette on the pumpkin; the whole village eating pumpkin soup at one long table; your cat asleep inside a pumpkin-shell house.]
> And the ending? First prize! The whole village eats pumpkin soup for a week, and your cat moves into the empty shell. It's only a story.

[Screen: "15, not 10!" — the 5 extras pop up highlighted. Then ONE screen: the ladder, exactly 15 rows, ONE row per word (no pairs), large first and ginormous last, each row = word · meaning · when to use. Heading: "big → extremely big". Never write "top" or "bottom" of the ladder in a cell.]

> And here's the sneaky part: I gave you fifteen words, not ten. Bulky, hefty, whopping, towering and mammoth were never on the list. Did any of them make it onto yours?

> Here's the whole ladder on one screen — roughly from big to extremely big. The order is a guide, not a rule.

> Large — big. For sizes and amounts: "a large coffee."
> Considerable — large in amount. Fairly formal: time, money, effort.
> Bulky — big and difficult to carry or move.
> Hefty — big and heavy. With money, a bit informal: "a hefty bill."
> Jumbo — larger than usual. Informal: for products, "a jumbo pack."
> Huge — extremely big. Very common in speech.
> Giant — much bigger than others of its kind. Before a noun.
> Enormous — extremely big. Fine anywhere.
> Massive — very big, heavy and solid.
> Whopping — very big. Informal, often before a number.
> Vast — extremely big: areas, spaces, amounts.
> Towering — very tall. A bit literary.
> Mammoth — extremely big. Often "a mammoth task."
> Immense — extremely big or great. Also for feelings.
> Ginormous — extremely big. Informal and playful.

Ladder caveat: the order is a teaching simplification. Steps 3–5 (bulky, hefty, jumbo) differ in type (awkward shape / heavy or costly / product size), not in strength. Steps 6–15 are all "extremely big" in the dictionaries; their order follows the story more than a strength scale. The voice says "roughly" and "a guide, not a rule". "The strongest word here" for ginormous is a teaching choice (its dictionary gloss equals huge/enormous); GATE: if this reads as a false claim, change to "The most playful word here."

Deliberately NOT used: great (mostly "very good" or "important" at B1; "a great deal/number" would need a separate scene), major (importance, not size), tremendous (degree/effect, and "very good" in informal speech), substantial/sizeable (too close to considerable), colossal and gigantic (too close to enormous/ginormous; the ladder already has ten "extremely big" words), monumental/titanic (figurative/importance).

Items in the dialogue to double-check: "a large number of people", "a considerable amount of time", "a hefty fine", "a jumbo pack of popcorn", "a huge mistake", "Giant pumpkin in Mill Lane!" (headline style, no article), "flat as a pancake", "a whopping twelve hundred kilos" (spoken 1,200 as "twelve hundred" in the gloss vs "one thousand two hundred" in the scene — both correct; GATE may prefer one), "the vast majority", "It's towering over the whole street", "a mammoth task", "immense relief", "It's ginormous!".

Stress cues (on screen only, optional in polish): TOW-er-ing, im-MENSE, jy-NOR-mous (GATE: check the respelling of ginormous /dʒaɪˈnɔːməs/; alternative: "gy-NOR-mous").

## What NOT to claim

- No memory statistics of any kind: no "most people remember 5 (or 5–7) of 10", no "the average person recalls…", no "X times faster", no "4x stronger connections", no "70% gone by day 3", no percentages.
- "Most people" is not used at all in the voice-over; the viewer's own two scores are the only evidence.
- Do not say the extras "came only/entirely from the story"; just ask whether they made it onto the list.
- Do not claim the story method makes words permanent or replaces review/using the words.
- Do not cite any research/researcher; do not say how old the method is.
- Do not name the source video, its channel, or any person/brand except anotherwordfor.net.
- Do not present the ladder as exact or official; it is approximate.
- Do not say jumbo, whopping or ginormous are fine in formal writing; do not call considerable informal.
- Do not teach massive = "very serious", towering = "impressive", or where "ginormous" comes from.
- Do not say "the top of the ladder" (the drawn table may run top-down), and do not call any word "the strongest".
- No pronunciation drills ("say it with me"), no spoken spelling-out of stress; stress cues are on screen only.
- Do not claim the site has example sentences or a search box.
- Do not call this a podcast/episode/show; no "welcome", "today we", sign-offs.
