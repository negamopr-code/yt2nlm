Checked and passing, with no change:
- **snag**: Oxford "(informal) a problem or difficulty, especially one that is small, hidden or unexpected"; Cambridge "informal a problem, difficulty, or disadvantage" → "a problem, often small, hidden or unexpected. Informal: "The only snag is…"". "The only snag: there's a tiny hole…" is natural spoken English.
- **hitch**: Oxford "a problem or difficulty that causes a short delay"; Cambridge "a temporary difficulty that causes a short delay", "go off without a hitch". "Without a hitch means with no problems at all" is correct. Not labelled.
- **hiccup** meaning: Oxford "a small problem or temporary delay"; Cambridge "a problem that delays or interrupts something for a while, but does not usually cause serious difficulties". The mixer scene fits. The body-noise sense is not taught or drawn.
- **glitch** meaning: "a small fault that stops something working properly" (Oxford "stops something working successfully"; Cambridge "prevents something from … working as well as it should"). A frozen GPS fits well.
- **obstacle**: Oxford senses 1+2; Cambridge "something that blocks your way so that movement or progress is prevented or made more difficult". A sofa stuck on the stairs fits exactly.
- **headache** meaning: Oxford "a person or thing that causes worry or trouble"; Cambridge "something that causes you great difficulty and worry". The parrot holding its head is a harmless visual pun; the pain sense is not taught.
- **trouble**: Oxford "a problem, worry, difficulty, etc."; "in trouble" = "a situation in which you can be criticized or punished"; Cambridge B1 "a situation in which you experience problems, usually because of something you have done wrong". "Someone is angry with you, or you may be punished" is a fair B1 gloss. No countability claim.
- **dilemma** (flagged): Oxford "…often one in which you have to make a very difficult choice between things of equal importance"; Cambridge B2 "a situation in which a difficult choice has to be made between two different things you could do", and sense 2 "…possibilities that will all have results you do not want". "A very difficult choice, often between two things. Not just any problem: a hard choice." plus the scene "Two options, and both are bad" is accurate and hedged ("often").
- **crisis**: Oxford "a time of great danger, difficulty or doubt when problems must be solved or important decisions must be made". The gloss is Oxford minus "doubt". A fire alarm at the party counts as a crisis in the story's light sense.
- **issue** meaning: Oxford sense 2 "a problem or worry that somebody has with something". The topic and magazine senses are not taught.
- **Ladder order** (hiccup → snag → hitch → glitch → issue → difficulty → obstacle → complication → setback → headache → trouble → dilemma → predicament → crisis → disaster): the four opening words are the ones both dictionaries mark as small or short ("small", "temporary", "short delay", "small fault"). The two closing words are the ones defined by "great danger", "great harm, damage, or death". The middle differs in TYPE as well as size, and the voice says "roughly" and "a guide, not a rule". Order holds as a rough ladder; no word is called "the worst" or "the strongest".
