# S5 — Synonym Memory Challenge: PROBLEM (script v1)

Language gate: APPROVED 2026-09-29 by glottos-language-editor (gate_text, see lang-gate-v1.md). Any later edit voids it.

(Written without a leading `>` on purpose: in this script `>` marks SPOKEN lines.)

Series: Synonym Memory Challenge · Special S5 · format: mid-length 16:9 video, whiteboard style, target 5–7 min.
Method (for us only, never in the voice-over): list test → pause → one continuous visual story → pause → "15, not 10" reveal → comment your two scores → try it with your own words.

Conventions:
- Lines starting with `>` are SPOKEN. Everything else (headings, [brackets], notes) is for us / the drawing, not spoken. Never read a [bracket] aloud.
- [EXTRA] marks the 5 "sneaky" extras that are in the story but NOT in the list. Never say "extra" in the story itself.
- [Stress cue] = ON-SCREEN text only, next to the word's caption. It is never spoken and never drilled (S1 lesson).
- Timings are approximate (0.9× reading pace, ~140 spoken words/min).
- Tone: light and silly. Nobody is hurt, no real people, no real brands. The story has a happy ending.
- ONE main character in every scene: "you" = the baker, same clothes all day (tall white chef's hat, red apron, blue trainers) — S2 lesson: the character changed between scenes. One running gag: a green parrot that rides on your shoulder and says "Uh-oh!".
- EVERY one of the 15 words gets its own picture AND its word + one-line meaning written on screen (S3 lesson: bulky/hefty had no picture; S4 lesson: the FIRST word, cute, had no picture for 32 s). Here the story set-up and the first word are ONE scene, drawn at once: the kitchen, you, the parrot, the mixer.
- Captions use exactly the register words of the voice (S3 lesson: the card said "Very literary" while the voice said "a bit literary"). In S5 only snag carries a label ("Informal"); hiccup, glitch and headache carry NONE (Oxford informal, Cambridge unlabelled → no claim).
- The target word is written in lower case in focus.txt and in spoken lines (S3 lesson: "BIG" in capitals was spelled out B-I-G).

Lessons from S1–S4 applied up front: no spoken pronunciation drills; no stage directions read aloud; the voice says "pause the video", never "pause the explainer" (S3); real silent holds on both pause cards, and the tip in Beat 4 comes AFTER a clear "Done?" (S4 v1: the tip talked over the writing moment; holds are planned for polish anyway); recap = exactly 15 rows, one row per word, no pairs, exactly the Beat 5 labels (S4 v1: the drawn ladder had only 8 of 15 rows); the reveal card writes the 5 extras (S3/S4: the voice named them, the card didn't); no "top/bottom of the ladder" and no "worst word" or "strongest word" in the voice, the ladder is described only as "from small problems to very serious ones", first drawn = hiccup, last drawn = disaster; the reveal makes no "came only from the story" claim; the comment line is one exact sentence ("Which score was higher? Comment your two scores: list versus story."); a transfer line ("tonight, try it with your own new words") before the CTA; tip "walk through the story again"; the ending line names the people ("Your grandmother…"), no ambiguous "it" (S4 v1 ending card); CTA = look up "another word for problem" on anotherwordfor.net (no search box, no example sentences on the site); "often", never "always".

## Beat 3 — Visual story: one birthday cake, 15 words, small problem → very serious problem (0:48–4:50)

[Screen: one continuous whiteboard story (a series of panels is fine, as long as the story runs on and "you" is the same baker in the tall white chef's hat, red apron and blue trainers); each word is written next to its picture as it is spoken, with its one-line meaning. The green parrot is in every scene.]

> Now the trick: pictures instead of a list. One story — just imagine every scene. The words go roughly from small problems to very serious ones.

### 1. hiccup [EXTRA]
[Your kitchen, morning: you in the chef's hat and red apron, the parrot on your shoulder, a big bowl and an electric mixer; the mixer stopping with a little "…" bubble, then whirring again; a calendar: "Grandma — 90!"]
> Hiccup. It's your grandmother's ninetieth birthday, and you are baking the cake, with your parrot on your shoulder. The mixer stops for a second, then starts again. Just a hiccup. Hiccup — a small problem or a short delay.

### 2. snag
[The cake tin in close-up: a tiny hole in the bottom, one drop of cake mixture falling; the parrot catching the drop in its beak.]
> Snag. The only snag: there's a tiny hole in the cake tin, and the cake mixture drips out, drop by drop. The parrot catches the drops. Snag — a problem, often small, hidden or unexpected. Informal: "The only snag is…"

### 3. hitch
[Outside: your little van; you pulling at the back door, which won't open; a clock showing five minutes passing; then the door flying open.]
> Hitch. The cake is ready. You carry it to your van, but the back door won't open. Five minutes later, it opens. A hitch — a problem that causes a short delay. And "without a hitch" means with no problems at all.

### 4. glitch [EXTRA]
[Inside the van: the GPS screen frozen, showing your van in the middle of the sea; a speech bubble from the GPS: "Turn left… left… left…"; the parrot covering its eyes with a wing.]
> Glitch. On the road, the GPS freezes. It says you're in the middle of the sea. Glitch — a small fault that stops something working properly. Often for machines and computers.

### 5. issue
[An old building; a manager with a clipboard at the door, pointing at a sign "NO ANIMALS"; the parrot on your shoulder; next panel: you hiding the parrot under your tall chef's hat.]
> Issue. At the old building, the manager says, "There's just one issue: no animals allowed." So the parrot hides under your chef's hat. Issue — a problem or worry that someone has with something. Often used at work: "If you have any issues, please call."

### 6. difficulty
[A long, narrow staircase; a sign "Party — 6th floor"; you carrying the huge cake, red in the face, knees shaking; the chef's hat moving on its own.]
> Difficulty. The party is on the sixth floor, and the cake is huge. You have difficulty carrying it up the narrow stairs. Difficulty — a problem, or the fact that something is hard to do. "I have difficulty carrying it."

### 7. obstacle
[Third floor: a huge sofa stuck across the stairs, blocking the way; you and the cake on one side; the parrot peeking out from under the hat.]
> Obstacle. On the third floor, a huge sofa is stuck on the stairs. You can't get past. Obstacle — something in your way that makes it hard to move forward, or to do what you want.

### 8. complication [EXTRA]
[The parrot flying out from under the hat and landing in the cream on top of the cake; feathers everywhere; the parrot saying "Uh-oh!".]
> Complication. Then the parrot flies out from under your hat — and lands in the cream. Now there are feathers on the cake. Complication — something that makes a situation more difficult.

### 9. setback
[You stepping on a feather, slipping; the top layer of the cake sliding down the stairs; next panel: you back in your kitchen, baking the top layer again; an arrow pointing back.]
> Setback. You step on a feather and slip. The top of the cake slides down the stairs. Back to the kitchen, and you bake it again. Setback — a problem that delays your progress, or makes things worse.

### 10. headache
[Your kitchen again: you at the oven, a phone ringing with many speech bubbles around it: "Who sits next to Grandma?", "Are there nuts in it?", "Can I bring my dog?"; the parrot holding its head with both wings.]
> Headache. While the cake is in the oven, your phone rings all afternoon: forty relatives, forty questions. What a headache! Headache — something that causes you a lot of worry and trouble.

### 11. trouble
[Back at the building: the manager with the clipboard, arms crossed, very angry, looking at the parrot sitting on the new cake; the parrot saying "Uh-oh!".]
> Trouble. Back at the building, the manager sees the parrot on the new cake. You're in trouble. Trouble — problems and worry. "In trouble" — someone is angry with you, or you may be punished.

### 12. dilemma
[You at the bottom of the stairs with the cake; two arrows: left = the stairs with the sofa still stuck on them; right = a tiny, old lift with a sign "MAX 1 PERSON"; a big question mark over your head.]
> Dilemma. The sofa is still on the stairs. So: climb over it with the cake, or take the tiny old lift? Two options, and both are bad. Dilemma — a very difficult choice, often between two things. Not just any problem: a hard choice.

### 13. predicament [EXTRA]
[Inside the tiny lift, stopped between floors 4 and 5; you squashed against the wall, the cake, the parrot; a clock: "Party starts in 5 minutes".]
> Predicament. You take the lift. It stops between the fourth and fifth floors. You, a huge cake and a parrot, and the party starts in five minutes. Predicament — an unpleasant situation that is hard to get out of, where it's difficult to know what to do.

### 14. crisis [EXTRA]
[The lift moving again; the party hall; ninety candles lit on the cake, a big cloud of smoke; the fire alarm ringing; all the guests turning to look at you; you with both hands up.]
> Crisis. The lift moves again. You light ninety candles. There's so much smoke that the fire alarm goes off. Everyone looks at you. Crisis — a time of great danger or difficulty, when important decisions must be made.

### 15. disaster
[The sprinklers in the ceiling turning on; water everywhere; the cake turning into cake soup; the guests wet, hair flat; nobody hurt.]
> Disaster. Then the water sprinklers come on. The cake turns into soup, and all the guests are wet. The party is a disaster. Disaster — a very bad situation, or a complete failure. Also a terrible event, like a flood or a fire.

### Happy ending (no new word)
[Your grandmother, soaked, laughing, eating cake soup from a big bowl with a spoon; all the guests with spoons; the parrot on her shoulder, a party hat on its head; a banner "90!".]
> And the ending? Your grandmother laughs and says, "Best birthday in ninety years!" Everyone eats cake soup with spoons. It's only a story.

## Beat 5 — Reveal + ladder recap (5:10–6:30)

[Screen: "15, not 10!" — the 5 extras written and highlighted (hiccup, glitch, complication, predicament, crisis). Then ONE screen: the ladder, exactly 15 rows, ONE row per word (no pairs), hiccup first and disaster last, each row = word · meaning · when to use. Heading: "small problem → very serious problem". > And here's the sneaky part: I gave you fifteen words, not ten. Hiccup, glitch, complication, predicament and crisis were never on the list. Did any of them make it onto yours?

> Here's the whole ladder on one screen — roughly from small problems to very serious ones. The order is a guide, not a rule.

> Hiccup — a small problem or a short delay.
> Snag — a problem, often small, hidden or unexpected. Informal.
> Hitch — a problem that causes a short delay.
> Glitch — a small fault that stops something working. Often machines and computers.
> Issue — a problem or worry. Often used at work.
> Difficulty — a problem. "I have difficulty doing it."
> Obstacle — something in your way.
> Complication — something that makes a situation more difficult.
> Setback — a problem that delays your progress.
> Headache — something that causes a lot of worry.
> Trouble — problems and worry. "In trouble": someone is angry with you.
> Dilemma — a very difficult choice, often between two things.
> Predicament — a difficult situation that is hard to get out of.
> Crisis — a time of great danger or difficulty.
> Disaster — a very bad situation or a complete failure. Also a flood or a fire.

## What NOT to claim

- No memory statistics of any kind: no "most people remember 5 (or 5–7) of 10", no "the average person recalls…", no "X times faster", no percentages.
- "Most people" is not used at all in the voice-over; the viewer's own two scores are the only evidence.
- Do not say the extras "came only/entirely from the story"; just ask whether they made it onto the list.
- Do not claim the story method makes words permanent or replaces review/using the words.
- Do not cite any research/researcher; do not say how old the method is.
- Do not name the source video, its channel, or any person/brand except anotherwordfor.net.
- Do not present the ladder as exact or official; it is approximate.
- Do not say dilemma means any problem; do not say glitch is "only" for computers; do not say trouble is "always uncountable".
- Do not call hitch, issue, obstacle, dilemma, predicament, crisis or disaster "formal" or "informal"; do not call snag "slang".
- Do not call hiccup, glitch or headache "informal" or "a bit informal" (Oxford labels them informal, Cambridge does not; GATE rule: no register claim when the dictionaries disagree). Snag is the only word with a label.
- Do not say "satnav" (British only); say "GPS".
- Do not teach hiccup as the body noise, headache as a pain in the head, complication as a medical term, issue as a magazine issue or a topic.
- Do not say "the top/bottom of the ladder", and do not call any word "the worst" or "the strongest".
- No pronunciation drills ("say it with me"), no spoken spelling-out; stress cues are on screen only (none are needed for this word set).
- Do not claim the site has example sentences or a search box.
- Do not call this a podcast/episode/show/explainer; no "welcome", "today we", sign-offs.
- Nobody is hurt: no fire drawn, only smoke and an alarm; the sprinklers make everyone wet, nothing more.
