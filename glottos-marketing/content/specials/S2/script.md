(Written without a leading `>` on purpose: in this script `>` marks SPOKEN lines.)

Series: Synonym Memory Challenge · Special S2 · format: mid-length 16:9 explainer, whiteboard style, target 5–7 min.
Method (for us only, never in the voice-over): list test → pause → one continuous visual story → pause → "15, not 10" reveal → comment your two scores → try it with your own words.

Conventions:
- Lines starting with `>` are SPOKEN. Everything else (headings, [brackets], notes) is for us / the drawing, not spoken. Never read a [bracket] aloud.
- [EXTRA] marks the 5 "sneaky" extras that are in the story but NOT in the list. Never say "extra" in the story itself.
- [Stress cue] = ON-SCREEN text only, next to the word's caption. It is never spoken and never drilled (S1 lesson: NotebookLM turned a spoken cue into a "say it with me" drill with the wrong stress).
- Timings are approximate (0.9× reading pace, ~140 spoken words/min).
- Tone: light and silly. Nobody dies, nobody is ill for real, no real people. The story has a happy ending.

Lessons from S1 applied up front: no spoken pronunciation drills; no stage directions read aloud; real silent holds on both pause cards (planned for polish anyway); recap = one row per word, no pairs, exactly the Beat 5 labels; the reveal makes no "came only from the story" claim; a transfer line ("try it with your own words tonight") before the CTA; CTA = look up "another word for sad" on anotherwordfor.net (no search box, no example sentences on the site).

## Beat 3 — Visual story: one scene, 15 words, a little sad → extremely sad (0:50–4:45)

[Screen: one continuous whiteboard story (it is fine if it is a series of panels, as long as the story runs on); each word is written next to its picture as it is spoken, with its one-line meaning.]

> Now the trick: pictures instead of a list. One story — just imagine every scene. It goes from a little bit sad to extremely sad, and so do the words. Don't worry: it ends well.

### 1. blue
[Grey sky at the window; you in blue pyjamas with a tiny blue rain cloud over your head.]
> Blue. It's your birthday. You look out at a grey sky and feel a little blue. Nothing is wrong — you're just not smiling yet. Blue — informal: a little sad. We usually say "I'm feeling a bit blue."

### 2. disappointed
[Postman handing over ONE envelope; the card says "Time for your check-up! — Your dentist".]
> Disappointed. You hoped for a pile of birthday cards. The postman brings one — from your dentist. You're disappointed. Disappointed — sad because something wasn't as good as you hoped.

### 3. glum [EXTRA]
[Breakfast table: you, chin in hand, long face, stirring cold cereal; the dog next to you with exactly the same long face.]
> Glum. At breakfast you stir your cereal, saying nothing. Your dog sits next to you with the same long face. You both look glum. Glum — quiet and a bit sad, and it shows on your face. A bit informal.

### 4. unhappy
[Oven open; a tall castle cake leaning to one side; poster on the wall "CAKE CONTEST TODAY"; you frowning at the cake.]
> Unhappy. Never mind — today is the big cake contest! You bake a tall castle cake. It comes out of the oven leaning to one side. You're unhappy with it. Unhappy — not happy. "Unhappy with" something means not satisfied: "The customer was unhappy with the service."

### 5. down in the dumps
[Rubbish dump: you sitting on a pile of bin bags, the leaning cake on your knees, a seagull next to you.]
> Down in the dumps. You take the cake to the dump to throw it away… and just sit there on the bin bags. You're down in the dumps — literally! It's an informal idiom: unhappy and low.

### 6. gloomy
[You pushing the cake in a wheelbarrow under huge black clouds; thought bubble: "It will rain. Nobody will like it. Nothing ever works."]
> Gloomy. You take the cake to the contest anyway. Under black clouds, you think: "It will rain. Nobody will like it. Nothing ever works." Gloomy — sad and without hope. We also say it about dark weather: "a gloomy day."

### 7. upset
[Rain pouring; a goose running off with the top tower of the cake; you with tears in your eyes, arms out.]
> Upset. Then it rains. And a goose grabs the top of your cake and runs away with it. Your eyes fill with tears. You're upset. Upset — unhappy because something bad has just happened. Very common in speech: "Don't be upset."

### 8. melancholy
[While the judges taste the cakes: you on a bench by a lake, playing a slow tune on a harmonica, leaves falling, drooping music notes.]
[Stress cue, on screen only: MEL-an-chol-y]
> Melancholy. While the judges taste the cakes, you sit by the lake playing a slow, melancholy tune on your harmonica, thinking about birthdays long ago. Melancholy — a quiet, deep sadness that stays with you, and you can't always say why. Literary: common in books and songs.

### 9. dejected [EXTRA]
[The judge — a very serious goat in a chef's hat — holding up a "0" card; you walking away, shoulders dropped, head down.]
> Dejected. The judge, a very serious goat, tastes your cake and holds up a card: zero. You walk away, head down. Dejected — unhappy and disappointed, especially after you tried and failed. Common in sports news: "the dejected players."

### 10. forlorn [EXTRA]
[Empty park in the rain; you alone on a wet bench holding half a cake and one balloon that is slowly going down.]
[Stress cue, on screen only: for-LORN]
> Forlorn. Everyone goes home. You sit alone on a wet bench in the empty park, holding half a cake and one sad balloon. Forlorn — alone and sad, as if nobody cares. A literary word.

### 11. miserable
[At home: you soaking wet in a blanket, sneezing, a broken heater with a sad face, a bowl of cold soup.]
> Miserable. At home you're soaking wet, the heating is broken, you're sneezing, and the soup is cold. Miserable — very unhappy, often because you're cold, wet, tired or uncomfortable. Everyday word — and we say "miserable weather" too.

### 12. sorrowful [EXTRA]
[Candle, feather pen, a long scroll titled "Oh Cake, My Cake"; you writing with a very dramatic sad face.]
> Sorrowful. By candlelight, you write a long, sorrowful poem: "Oh cake, my beautiful cake…" Sorrowful — very sad. Old-fashioned and literary: for poems and old stories, not for a chat with friends.

### 13. heartbroken
[Your window: next door, your dog sits happily with the neighbour's cat; you pressing your face to the glass; a cracked heart above your head.]
> Heartbroken. Then, through the window, you see your dog living next door with the neighbour's cat. You're heartbroken. Heartbroken — extremely sad, usually because someone or something you love is gone.

### 14. devastated
[Storm: your roof flying away like a hat, the dentist's card flying with it; you standing in the room, shocked, mouth open.]
> Devastated. Suddenly a storm blows your roof away like a hat — with your one birthday card. You stand there, shocked. Devastated — extremely upset and shocked when something very bad happens. Common in speech too: "We were devastated when we lost the final."

### 15. inconsolable [EXTRA]
[You crying a river that flows out of the door and down the street; neighbours offering tissues, a new cake, a phone with a funny cat video; you shaking your head.]
[Stress cue, on screen only: in-con-SOLE-a-ble]
> Inconsolable. You cry a river, out of the door and down the street. Your neighbours bring tissues, a new cake, a funny cat video — nothing helps. Inconsolable — so sad that nobody can make you feel better. The top of the ladder.

### Happy ending (no new word)
[Your dog walking back in through the door, with the cat, carrying a new cake with a candle; you smiling.]
> And then… your dog comes home, with the cat and a brand-new birthday cake. It's only a story.

## Beat 5 — Reveal + ladder recap (5:05–6:25)

[Screen: "15, not 10!" — the 5 extras pop up highlighted. Then ONE screen: the ladder, bottom = blue, top = inconsolable, ONE row per word (no pairs), each row = word · meaning · when to use.]

> And here's the sneaky part: I gave you fifteen words, not ten. Glum, dejected, forlorn, sorrowful and inconsolable were never on the list. Did any of them make it onto yours?

> Here's the whole ladder on one screen — roughly from a little bit sad to extremely sad. The order is a guide, not a rule.

> Blue — a little sad. Informal: "feeling blue."
> Disappointed — sad because something wasn't as good as you hoped. Fine anywhere.
> Glum — quiet and a bit sad. Informal: "a glum face."
> Unhappy — not happy, or not satisfied. Neutral, fine at work.
> Down in the dumps — unhappy and low. Informal: with friends.
> Gloomy — sad and without hope. Also for dark weather.
> Upset — unhappy because something bad just happened. Very common in speech.
> Melancholy — a quiet, deep sadness that stays. Literary: books and songs.
> Dejected — disappointed after trying and failing. Often in sports news.
> Forlorn — alone and sad. Literary.
> Miserable — very unhappy or uncomfortable. Everyday; also for weather.
> Sorrowful — very sad. Old-fashioned and literary: poems and old stories.
> Heartbroken — extremely sad: someone or something you love is gone.
> Devastated — extremely upset and shocked. Speech and news.
> Inconsolable — nobody can make you feel better. The top.

Ladder caveat: the order is a teaching simplification. 3–6 (glum, unhappy, down in the dumps, gloomy) differ more in type (quiet / not satisfied / low energy / no hope) than in strength; 8, 10 and 12 (melancholy, forlorn, sorrowful) are literary flavours rather than steps. 13–15 are all "extremely sad". The voice-over says "roughly" and "the order is a guide, not a law".

Deliberately NOT used: depressed (a medical condition as well as an everyday word; too easy to present wrongly), grief-stricken / mourning / bereaved (death), crushed, down/low (too close to "down in the dumps"), fed up (more "annoyed and bored" than sad).

Items in the dialogue to double-check: "I'm feeling a bit blue." / "feeling blue", "It will rain. Nobody will like it. Nothing ever works." (gloomy), "Don't be upset.", "the dejected players", "a glum face", "The customer was unhappy with the service.", "We were devastated when we lost the final.", "Oh cake, my beautiful cake…" (sorrowful poem), "a slow, sad tune on your harmonica" (melancholy).

## What NOT to claim

- No memory statistics of any kind: no "most people remember 5 (or 5–7) of 10", no "the average person recalls…", no "X times faster", no "4x stronger connections", no "70% gone by day 3", no percentages.
- "Most people" is not used at all in the voice-over; the viewer's own two scores are the only evidence.
- Do not say the extras "came only/entirely from the story" (a viewer may already know them); just ask whether they made it onto the list.
- Do not claim the story method makes words permanent or replaces review/using the words.
- Do not claim "visual memory" and "verbal memory" are separate brain systems or cite any research/researcher; do not say how old the method is.
- Do not name the source video, its channel, or any person/brand except anotherwordfor.net.
- Do not present the ladder as exact or official; it is approximate.
- Do not say blue / down in the dumps are fine in formal writing; do not say melancholy, forlorn or sorrowful are casual.
- No talk of depression, serious illness (a sneeze is fine), death or grief of real people; no mental-health advice.
- No pronunciation drills ("say it with me"), no spoken spelling-out of stress; stress cues are on screen only.
- Do not claim the site has example sentences or a search box.
- Do not call this a podcast/episode/show; no "welcome", "today we", sign-offs.
