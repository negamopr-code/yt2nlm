## Beat 2 — Why new words disappear (0:35–1:32)

[Whiteboard: a curve that drops steeply, then flattens. NO numbers on the axes, only "time" and "remembered". Beside it, nonsense syllable cards "DAX" and "BOK" with a question mark. Then a second, flatter curve labelled "words with meaning". Then a lonely card "livid = very angry" floating in empty space, with a small broom sweeping it away.]

> Here's what happens. Right after you learn something, memory drops fast. A lot is lost in the first hours and days, and then the loss slows down.

> This curve was first measured with meaningless syllables, like "dax" and "bok", with nothing to connect them to. Things that have meaning fade more slowly. And that's the key: the more things a word is attached to, the longer it lasts.

> Now look at how most of us learn words. "Livid equals very angry." That's a label with nothing to hold it in place. Your brain treats it as noise and clears it out.

> So the real question is not "How do I push more words in?" It's "What can I attach them to?" Here are three easy steps. The first one is the strongest.

## Beat 3 — Step 1 (LEAD): use the word, say it and write it (1:32–2:47)

[Whiteboard: big "1 USE IT" with a pencil and a speech bubble. Two arrows between a word card and a head: "recognise: word → meaning" (thin arrow) and "use: meaning → word" (thick arrow). Then a person mid-conversation with an empty thought bubble ("blank"). Then QUOTE 2 on a sheet of paper. Then a notebook page with the handwritten sentence "I was swamped at work all week." and a mouth icon "say it out loud ×1", then "two new sentences, to a friend".]

> Step one: use the word. Say it, and write it.

> In thousands of learner comments under videos about remembering words, this is the method people most often say works for them. They didn't just look at new words. They used them.

> Why does it work? Reading a word trains recognition: "Oh yes, I know that one." Using it trains the other direction: finding the word when you need it. And that's exactly the skill that's missing when you understand everything, but your mind goes blank as soon as you speak.

> A learner wrote: "Writing down everything helps me the most because my eyes and mind have seen and read it on paper."

> So here's what to do. For each new word, write one sentence about your own life. Not a textbook sentence. Yours. "I was swamped at work all week." Then say it out loud. A few days later, say two new sentences with the same word, as if you were telling a friend.

> And keep it small. Three new words a day is plenty.

## Beat 4 — Step 2: learn the word inside a sentence (2:47–4:20)

[Whiteboard: "2 IN A SENTENCE". Left: the lonely card "livid = very angry". Right: a small scene, an airport board with "CANCELLED" three times, a man with steam coming out of his ears; the sentence written underneath. Four hook icons appear around the word: person, situation, feeling, reason. Then QUOTE 3 on a card. Then a "when to use it" card for "swamped" (fine at work ✓ / one small task ✗) and for "livid" (extremely angry ✓ / a little annoyed ✗).]

> Step two: never learn a word alone. Learn it inside a sentence.

> Compare these two. "Livid: very angry." Now this: "He was livid when he found out his flight had been cancelled, for the third time." Suddenly the word has a person, a situation, a feeling and a reason. Four hooks to hold it in place.

> And when you need the word in a real conversation, you don't have to build a sentence from nothing. You already have one ready.

> A learner wrote: "I must say that 'repeat the sentence I made myself over and over' was a game changer for me."

> Notice the words: "the sentence I made myself". Reading a good sentence once is a start. Making your own sentence and saying it is what makes the word yours.

> Learn one more thing with every word: when to use it. Many learners freeze, not because they forgot the word, but because they're not sure it's right for the situation. Is it too formal? Too strong? So write down when to use it, and when to avoid it.

> "Swamped" is fine at work: "I'm completely swamped with reports this week." But avoid it when you only have one or two small tasks. "Livid" means extremely angry, so it's too strong when someone is just a little annoyed.

## Beat 5 — Step 3: timing, cover and recall on days 1, 3 and 7 (4:20–5:35)

[Whiteboard: "3 TIMING". Two stick-figure groups at desks with the same clock. Group A re-reads a page (eyes on page). Group B covers the page with a hand and looks up, thinking. Arrow "one week later": two bars, A "about 40%", B "about 61%", caption "of the text they studied". Then a hand covering the right column of "very busy → ____". Then a calendar with three circled days: 1 "see it in its sentence", 3 "cover it, recall it", 7 "2 new sentences, out loud". The forgetting curve from Beat 2 returns and gets flatter after each circled day.]

> Step three is about how and when you review. Re-reading your word list feels like studying. But it mostly trains recognition. Pulling a word out of your head works better than pushing it in again.

> In one test, students studied a short text. A week later, the ones who practised recalling it remembered about sixty-one percent of it. The ones who only re-read it remembered about forty percent. Same amount of time, a much better result.

> So cover the answer, and make your brain find it. That little moment of effort, "Wait, it's... swamped!", is exactly when the memory gets stronger.

> And do it at the right moments. The one, three, seven rule. Day one: see the word again, in its sentence. Day three: cover it, and recall it before you look. Day seven: use it in two new sentences of your own, out loud.

> Each review brings the word back before it's gone, and each time, the curve falls more slowly. Not every day. Not just once. Just days one, three and seven.

## Beat 6 — The 15-minute routine (5:35–6:07)

[Whiteboard: a clock face split into three 5-minute slices: 0–5 "ANCHOR: 3 new words, 1 sentence each", 5–10 "REVIEW: day-1 words in their sentences, day-3 words covered", 10–15 "SPEAK: day-7 words, 2 new sentences out loud". Crossed-out icons: phone app, streak flame.]

> Put it together, and it takes fifteen minutes a day.

> For the first five minutes: three new words, and one sentence about your own life for each. For the next five: read your day-one words in their sentences, then cover your day-three words and recall them. For the last five: say your day-seven words out loud, in two new sentences each.

> No app needed. No streak to protect. Just sentences, recall and your own voice.

## Accuracy notes (for glottos-language-editor)

- Forgetting curve (Beat 2): shape only, no percentages. The article's 56/66/75% are Ebbinghaus savings scores, and the language editor has already flagged "3 of 4 words gone" as an overclaim. So the voice says "drops fast, then slows down" and "measured with meaningless syllables… things that have meaning fade more slowly" (article §1). No name, no year.
- Re-read vs recall (Beat 5): about 40% vs about 61% a week later, same time, a TEXT (prose), not words (article §3; ep02 rule "never say 40% of words"). The figures are sourced (article §3, Roediger & Karpicke 2006, Exp. 2: repeated study ≈40% vs repeated recall ≈61% after one week, equal study time) and no one is named in the voice; reworded to "In one test, students studied a short text."

## What NOT to claim (steered in focus.txt too)

- No memory statistics other than the one about 40% / about 61% pair. No "4x stronger connections", no "70% gone by day 3", no "90% in a week", no "you'll forget 3 of 4 words", no percentages on the forgetting curve.
- No researcher names, universities, years, or "science says" / "proven".
- Do not claim that reading a word in context once gives fluent speech without producing it yourself (strategy, Episode 2).
- Do not claim any step makes words permanent, or that the 1·3·7 rule is a study result.
- Do not call re-reading useless. It "mostly trains recognition".
- No app or brand names (no Anki), no other channels, videos or people, no learner names.
- No podcast/episode/show words, no "welcome", "today we", "let's dive in", no sign-off. Start with the hook, end with the CTA.
- Do not say "naked" or "literally".
