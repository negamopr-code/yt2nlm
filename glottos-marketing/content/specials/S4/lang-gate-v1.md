Checked and passing, with no change:
- **cute**: Oxford "pretty and attractive", Cambridge "(especially of something or someone small or young) pleasant and attractive" → "nice to look at, for babies, small animals and small things". The NAmE informal "sexually attractive" and "clever" senses are not taught. No register label (the "(British English)" on the Oxford page belongs to one example, not to the sense).
- **pretty**: Oxford "(used most often about a woman or girl) attractive without being very beautiful", and for things "without being large, beautiful or impressive"; Cambridge "attractive or pleasant in a delicate way". "Not as strong as beautiful" and "We use it for girls and women, and for small things" are correct. The script says "we use it for", not "only".
- **picturesque**: Oxford "(of a place, building, scene, etc.) pretty, especially in a way that looks old-fashioned"; Cambridge "(especially of a place)" → "pretty, often in an old-fashioned way. For places and views." Not used for people. The stress cue pic-tur-ESQUE is real spelling, on screen only (S3 kept the same kind of cue), so it is not a respelling.
- **lovely**: Oxford headword "(especially British English)", Cambridge "mainly UK" → "very common in British English". No formality label is spoken. Oxford marks the "very pleasant" sense (informal) but Cambridge does not, so the script rightly says nothing. "What a lovely day!" and "You look lovely." are idiomatic.
- **attractive**: Oxford sense 1 "pleasant to look at" (people) and sense 2 (things, e.g. "an attractive garden"); Cambridge "very pleasing in appearance". "A neutral word for people and things, fine in writing too" is OK. The offer/sexual nuance is not taught.
- **good-looking**: Oxford "(especially of people) physically attractive", no label; Cambridge also allows products. "An everyday word for people, men and women" is OK (brief: informal-neutral).
- **gorgeous**: Oxford "(informal) very beautiful and attractive; giving great pleasure", with the examples "gorgeous weather" and "a gorgeous view"; Cambridge unlabelled, with the examples "What a gorgeous room/dress/colour!". "A bit informal, and very common when you speak" follows the S3 "hefty" precedent. "That's gorgeous!" about a cake is natural.
- **breathtaking**: Oxford "very exciting or impressive (usually in a pleasant way)", with the example "a breathtaking view of the mountains"; Cambridge "extremely exciting, beautiful, or surprising". "So beautiful that it takes your breath away. We often use it for views" is OK ("often", not "always").
