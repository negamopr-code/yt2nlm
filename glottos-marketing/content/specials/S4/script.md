# S4 — Synonym Memory Challenge: BEAUTIFUL (script v1)

Language gate: APPROVED 2026-09-28 by glottos-language-editor (report: lang-gate-v1.md).

(Written without a leading `>` on purpose: in this script `>` marks SPOKEN lines.)

Series: Synonym Memory Challenge · Special S4 · format: mid-length 16:9 video, whiteboard style, target 5–7 min.
Method (for us only, never in the voice-over): list test → pause → one continuous visual story → pause → "15, not 10" reveal → comment your two scores → try it with your own words.

Conventions:
- Lines starting with `>` are SPOKEN. Everything else (headings, [brackets], notes) is for us / the drawing, not spoken. Never read a [bracket] aloud.
- [EXTRA] marks the 5 "sneaky" extras that are in the story but NOT in the list. Never say "extra" in the story itself.
- [Stress cue] = ON-SCREEN text only, next to the word's caption. It is never spoken and never drilled (S1 lesson).
- Timings are approximate (0.9× reading pace, ~140 spoken words/min).
- Tone: light and silly. Nobody is hurt, no real people, no real brands. The story has a happy ending.
- ONE main character in every scene: "you" = the photographer, same clothes all day (yellow raincoat, green wellies, a big camera round the neck) — S2 lesson: the character changed between scenes. One running gag: a baby goat that follows you everywhere.
- EVERY one of the 15 words gets its own picture AND its word + one-line meaning written on screen (S3 lesson: bulky/hefty had no picture, whopping had no word on screen).
- Captions use exactly the register words of the voice (S3 lesson: the card said "Very literary" while the voice said "a bit literary").
- The target word is written in lower case in focus.txt and in spoken lines (S3 lesson: "BIG" in capitals was spelled out B-I-G).

Lessons from S1–S3 applied up front: no spoken pronunciation drills; no stage directions read aloud; the voice says "pause the video", never "pause the explainer" (S3); real silent holds on both pause cards (planned for polish anyway); recap = exactly 15 rows, one row per word, no pairs, exactly the Beat 5 labels; no "top/bottom of the ladder" and no "strongest word" in the voice; the reveal makes no "came only from the story" claim; the comment line is one exact sentence ("Which score was higher? Comment your two scores: list versus story."); a transfer line ("tonight, try it with your own new words") before the CTA; tip "walk through the story again"; CTA = look up "another word for beautiful" on anotherwordfor.net (no search box, no example sentences on the site); "often", never "always" / "almost always" (S3: whopping was stretched to "almost always").

## Beat 3 — Visual story: one mountain wedding, 15 words, nice to look at → extremely beautiful (0:50–4:50)

[Screen: one continuous whiteboard story (a series of panels is fine, as long as the story runs on and "you" is the same photographer in the yellow raincoat and green wellies); each word is written next to its picture as it is spoken, with its one-line meaning. The baby goat is in every scene.]

> Now the trick: pictures instead of a list. One story — just imagine every scene. The words go roughly from nice to look at to extremely beautiful.

### 1. cute
[A tiny mountain railway station; you with your big camera; a baby goat jumping out of a pile of hay into your open camera bag, only its head and big eyes showing.]
> Cute. Your best friend is getting married in a mountain village, and you are the photographer. At the little station, a baby goat jumps into your camera bag. Cute — nice to look at, for babies, small animals and small things: "a cute puppy".

### 2. pretty
[Village square: a six-year-old flower girl in a pink dress with a basket of flowers; the goat, still in your bag, eating a daisy from her basket.]
> Pretty. In the square, you meet the flower girl in a pretty pink dress. The goat eats a flower from her basket. Pretty — attractive, but not as strong as beautiful. We use it for girls and women, and for small things: "a pretty dress".

### 3. picturesque [EXTRA]
[The whole village drawn like a postcard: old stone houses, flowers in every window, a little stone bridge over a river, snowy mountains behind; you taking a photo; a postcard frame around the scene.]
[Stress cue, on screen only: pic-tur-ESQUE]
> Picturesque. Your first photo: old stone houses, flowers in every window, a little bridge, mountains behind. It looks like a painting. Picturesque — pretty, often in an old-fashioned way. For places and views: "a picturesque village".

### 4. lovely
[The bride's grandmother on a bench in the sun, the goat on her lap, arms open to the sky; speech bubble "What a lovely day!"; the last raindrops of the morning stopping.]
> Lovely. The morning rain stops, and the sun comes out. The bride's grandmother holds the goat and says: "What a lovely day!" Lovely — beautiful and very pleasant. Very common in British English: "You look lovely."

### 5. attractive
[Restaurant garden: long tables with white cloths, candles and little flowers; a proud waiter; the goat walking down the middle of one table, leaving little hoof prints.]
> Attractive. The waiters make the garden tables look attractive: white cloths, candles, little flowers. Attractive — pleasant to look at. A neutral word for people and things, fine in writing too.

### 6. good-looking
[The groom's twin brothers in matching suits, checking their hair in the reflection of your camera lens; the goat checking its little beard too.]
> Good-looking. The groom's twin brothers arrive. They're very good-looking, and they know it: they check their hair in your camera lens. Good-looking — attractive. An everyday word for people, men and women.

### 7. handsome
[The groom arriving on a white horse with a shiny mane; both of them posing; the goat looking up at the horse.]
> Handsome. Then the groom arrives on a white horse. A handsome man on a handsome horse! Handsome — good-looking. Usually for men; for a woman, it means attractive in a strong way. Also for horses and buildings: "a handsome horse".

### 8. elegant
[The bride's mother stepping out of an old car in a simple long blue dress and a small hat; nothing extra; the goat holding the car door open with its head.]
> Elegant. The bride's mother gets out of an old car in a simple long blue dress. Elegant — attractive and stylish. For people, clothes and places: "an elegant dress".

### 9. graceful [EXTRA]
[By the lake, guests waiting for the ceremony: a white swan gliding past, smooth and slow; the goat trying to copy it and falling into the water, SPLASH; guests laughing; the goat fine, just wet.]
> Graceful. By the lake, while everyone waits for the ceremony, a swan glides past, smooth and slow. The goat tries to copy it and falls in with a big splash — it's fine, just wet. Graceful — moving in a smooth, beautiful way. For dancers, swans and cats.

### 10. exquisite
[Close-up: the open ring box in the goat's mouth; two gold rings with tiny, perfect flowers cut into them; your camera zoomed in; a magnifying glass.]
> Exquisite. Now, where are the rings? The goat has the box in its mouth. You open it: two gold rings with tiny, perfect flowers on them. Exquisite — very beautiful and delicate, made with great care: "exquisite jewellery", "exquisite lace".

### 11. striking [EXTRA]
[The bride's aunt arriving late with bright red hair and a huge green hat shaped like a leaf; every head turns towards her, the goat's head too; the goat trying to eat the hat.]
> Striking. The bride's aunt arrives late, with bright red hair and a huge green hat. Every head turns — even the goat's. Striking — very attractive, often in an unusual way, so you notice it at once: "striking red hair".

### 12. radiant [EXTRA]
[The bride walking down the path by the lake, a huge happy smile, little lines of light drawn around her face like sunshine.]
> Radiant. Then the bride walks down the path. She's so happy that her face seems to glow. She looks radiant. Radiant — so happy or healthy that you seem to glow. Often for faces and smiles: "a radiant smile".

### 13. gorgeous
[The party: a wedding cake taller than the groom, with chocolate and flowers; guests with speech bubbles "That's gorgeous!"; the goat staring at the cake.]
> Gorgeous. At the party, the waiters bring in a cake taller than the groom, with chocolate and flowers. Everyone says: "That's gorgeous!" Gorgeous — very beautiful. A bit informal, and very common when you speak: "a gorgeous dress", "gorgeous weather".

### 14. stunning
[Sunset on the old stone bridge: the whole wedding party posing, the sky orange and pink; your camera screen showing the photo, with a small goat face in the corner.]
> Stunning. At sunset, everyone stands on the old bridge for a group photo. The sky turns orange and pink, and the photo is stunning. Stunning — extremely beautiful. Very common for people, clothes and views: "You look stunning."

### 15. breathtaking [EXTRA]
[Night: the goat running up a hill, everyone following with lanterns; at the top, the whole valley, the village lights and fireworks over the lake; everyone silent, mouths open.]
> Breathtaking. At night, the goat runs up a hill, and everyone follows with lanterns. At the top: the whole valley, the village lights and fireworks over the lake. The view is breathtaking. Breathtaking — so beautiful that it takes your breath away. We often use it for views: "a breathtaking view".

### Happy ending (no new word)
[The wedding album: the best photo of the day = the goat in the middle with the bride's flowers in its mouth; the couple hugging the goat; a sign on its little house: "OUR GOAT".]
> And the ending? The best photo of the day is the goat, with the bride's flowers in its mouth. The bride and groom take the goat home. It's only a story.

## Beat 5 — Reveal + ladder recap (5:10–6:30)

[Screen: "15, not 10!" — the 5 extras pop up highlighted (picturesque, graceful, striking, radiant, breathtaking). Then ONE screen: the ladder, exactly 15 rows, ONE row per word (no pairs), cute first and breathtaking last, each row = word · meaning · when to use. Heading: "nice to look at → extremely beautiful". Never write "top" or "bottom" of the ladder in a cell.]

> And here's the sneaky part: I gave you fifteen words, not ten. Picturesque, graceful, striking, radiant and breathtaking were never on the list. Did any of them make it onto yours?

> Here's the whole ladder on one screen — roughly from nice to look at to extremely beautiful. The order is a guide, not a rule.

> Cute — nice to look at. Babies, small animals, small things.
> Pretty — attractive, but not as strong as beautiful. Girls, women, small things.
> Picturesque — pretty, often in an old-fashioned way. Places and views.
> Lovely — beautiful and very pleasant. Very common in British English.
> Attractive — pleasant to look at. Neutral: people and things.
> Good-looking — attractive. An everyday word for men and women.
> Handsome — good-looking. Usually for men; also "a handsome horse."
> Elegant — attractive and stylish. People, clothes, places.
> Graceful — moving in a smooth, beautiful way. Dancers, swans, cats.
> Exquisite — very beautiful and delicate. Small things made with great care.
> Striking — very attractive, often in an unusual way. You notice it at once.
> Radiant — glowing with happiness or health. Faces and smiles.
> Gorgeous — very beautiful. A bit informal, very common when you speak.
> Stunning — extremely beautiful. People, clothes, views.
> Breathtaking — so beautiful that it takes your breath away. Often for views.

## Accuracy notes (for glottos-language-editor)

Meanings and labels were written from memory by the scenario writer and then VERIFIED by the language gate on 2026-09-28 against Oxford Learner's and Cambridge (both fetched live); all former GATE questions are resolved in the table below (see lang-gate-v1.md). The BEAUTIFUL family differs more by WHO/WHAT it describes than by strength, so the ladder is a rough nice-to-look-at → extremely-beautiful order with type words (places, movement, detail, happiness) in the middle; the voice says "roughly" and "a guide, not a rule".

| # | word | acted as | core meaning used | applies to / register note |

| 1 | cute | a baby goat in your camera bag | (esp. of something small or young) pretty and attractive | babies, small animals, small things. Neutral/everyday. We do NOT teach the informal NAmE "sexually attractive" sense. No register label is spoken. |
| 2 | pretty | a flower girl's pink dress | (of a woman/girl) attractive without being very beautiful; (of things/places) attractive but not large or impressive | girls, women, small things ("a pretty dress"). "Not as strong as beautiful" = Oxford's "without being very beautiful". We avoid saying it is never for men/boys (it can be, with a meaning of 'girlish'); the voice says "We use it for…", not "only". |
| 3 | picturesque | the stone village like a painting/postcard | (of a place, building, scene) pretty, especially in an old-fashioned way | places and views. No register label given (none in Oxford/Cambridge). Stress cue pic-tur-ESQUE is real spelling, on screen only (S3 precedent). |

Ladder caveat: rows 3 (picturesque), 8 (elegant), 9 (graceful), 10 (exquisite), 11 (striking) and 12 (radiant) differ in TYPE (places / style / movement / fine detail / unusual / happiness), not in strength; the order follows the story. Rows 13–15 are the strong end, but no word is called "the strongest" (dictionary glosses of stunning and breathtaking are both "extremely/very impressive").

Deliberately NOT used: ravishing (dated/literary, rare in speech — not teachable for B1 use), drop-dead gorgeous (informal and about sexual attractiveness; risky register for learners), dazzling (mainly "very bright" / "very impressive", beauty sense is secondary), magnificent (mainly "impressive/grand", overlaps stunning/breathtaking), charming (about pleasant manner more than looks), scenic (overlaps picturesque; mainly "a scenic route"), fair/comely/pulchritudinous/beauteous (archaic or literary), fine/nice/wonderful (not specifically about looks).

Items in the dialogue to double-check: "a cute puppy", "a pretty pink dress", "Your first photo: old stone houses…" (verbless, spoken style), "flowers in every window", "What a lovely day!", "You look lovely.", "make the garden tables look attractive", "They're very good-looking, and they know it", "A handsome man on a handsome horse!", "glides past, smooth and slow", "falls in with a big splash — it's fine, just wet", "exquisite jewellery", "Every head turns — even the goat's.", "her face seems to glow", "so happy or healthy that you seem to glow", "a radiant smile", "a cake taller than the groom", "That's gorgeous!", "gorgeous weather", "You look stunning", "it takes your breath away", "The bride and groom take the goat home." (gate replaced "The happy couple take it home": "it" was ambiguous between the photo and the goat, and "couple take" looks like an error to learners).

## What NOT to claim

- No memory statistics of any kind: no "most people remember 5 (or 5–7) of 10", no "the average person recalls…", no "X times faster", no percentages.
- "Most people" is not used at all in the voice-over; the viewer's own two scores are the only evidence.
- Do not say the extras "came only/entirely from the story"; just ask whether they made it onto the list.
- Do not claim the story method makes words permanent or replaces review/using the words.
- Do not cite any research/researcher; do not say how old the method is.
- Do not name the source video, its channel, or any person/brand except anotherwordfor.net.
- Do not present the ladder as exact or official; it is approximate.
- Do not say pretty/handsome are "only" for one sex; say "usually" / "we use it for".
- Do not call lovely or cute "informal" or "only British"; do not call exquisite "formal"; do not call gorgeous "very informal" or slang; do not call stunning "neutral" or "fine anywhere" (Oxford: rather informal); do not say elegant means "simple".
- Do not teach radiant as "bright light", graceful as "polite", handsome as "a lot of money", exquisite as "very painful", cute as "sexy".
- Do not say "the top of the ladder", and do not call any word "the strongest".
- No pronunciation drills ("say it with me"), no spoken spelling-out; stress cues are on screen only.
- Do not claim the site has example sentences or a search box.
- Do not call this a podcast/episode/show/explainer; no "welcome", "today we", sign-offs.
